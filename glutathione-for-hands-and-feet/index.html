Glutathione for hands and feet tan: what actually helps

Why hands and feet tan so fast in India, how to protect them, and what a glutathione cream can realistically do for the body.

Does glutathione remove tan from hands and feet?

Glutathione creams were studied on the face, not hands or feet, and the changes were modest. Tan on hands and feet fades mostly by itself once you stop new sun exposure, so daily sunscreen, gloves and socks matter far more than any cream.

How do I remove tan from my hands and feet?

Protect them from new sun: broad-spectrum SPF 30+ on the backs of hands and tops of feet every morning, reapplied after washing, plus gloves when riding and closed shoes or socks outdoors. Moisturise daily. Tan usually looks lighter over 4 to 8 weeks.

Can I use glutathione face cream on my hands?

You can use a little on the backs of the hands, but a 30 g face cream like the GlutaNex Night Cream is sized for the face and neck. For hands and feet, a good sunscreen and a plain body moisturiser are more practical and do more.

Why hands and feet tan so quickly

  • Two-wheeler riding: the backs of the hands face the sun on every commute, and the feet in sandals or floaters get the rest.
  • Sandals and chappals: the classic strap-line tan on the top of the foot comes from daily sun on the same uncovered skin.
  • Washing off sunscreen: hands get washed many times a day, so even people who apply sunscreen lose it within an hour.
  • Driving: UVA rays pass through car windows, so the right arm and hand often tan more.
  • Dry, thick skin on knuckles, heels and the tops of feet can look darker and dull.
  • Housework and chemicals: detergents and dishwashing strip moisture from hands, which makes them look rougher and darker.

Sun protection is the main step

  1. Sunscreen on hands and feet every morning: the backs of the hands, between the fingers, the wrists, and the tops of the feet. Use the same broad-spectrum SPF 30+ you use on your face, or a body sunscreen.
  2. Reapply after washing hands when you are going out, and before a long ride. Keep a small tube in your bag or bike storage.
  3. Riding gloves with full fingers cover the area that tans most. Cotton gloves are cooler in summer.
  4. Socks and closed shoes for long outdoor days, or at least sunscreen on the tops of the feet when you wear sandals.
  5. Arm sleeves (UV sleeves) for daily riders, especially in summer.

Tan is the skin's response to sun. Once new exposure stops, tan usually looks lighter over a few weeks by itself. That is why protection does most of the work. Our summer tan guide has more ideas for Indian summers.

Dry knuckles, cracked heels and dull skin

Much of what looks like dark hands and feet is actually dryness and thickened skin. Rough knuckles and heels reflect less light and look darker, and cracks can leave marks as they heal.

  • Hands: moisturise after every wash at home, and wear rubber gloves for dishes, laundry and cleaning. Detergents strip the skin more than most people realise.
  • Knuckles: a thicker cream or petroleum jelly at night, especially in winter.
  • Heels: soak feet in lukewarm water for ten minutes, pat dry, and apply a thick cream or petroleum jelly; wear cotton socks overnight. A gentle foot file once a week is fine, but avoid harsh scraping or blades.
  • Tops of feet: care for them like the face: sunscreen when exposed, moisturiser at night.

When hands and feet are well moisturised and protected, many people find they look noticeably more even within a few weeks, without any brightening product. For tan and spots on the face, see glutathione for dark spots and tan.

Using a glutathione cream on the body

Studies on glutathione creams looked at facial skin and showed modest changes in melanin (Khanna et al., J Clin Aesthet Dermatol 2025). There is no good research on hands or feet, where the skin is thicker and gets far more sun and washing.

There is also a practical point. GlutaNex Glutathione Night Cream is a face night cream in a 30 g jar, sized for nightly use on the face and neck. Spreading it over both hands and feet would use it up very quickly and is not what it is made for. It also contains retinol and salicylic acid, which make skin more sensitive to sun, and hands and feet are exactly the areas that get the most sun.

So for larger areas, the honest advice is simple: focus on sunscreen, gloves, socks and a good plain body moisturiser. If you want to use a little night cream on the backs of the hands at night, that is reasonable after a patch test, as long as you use sunscreen there every morning. For underarms, knees, elbows and other body areas, see glutathione for underarms and body.

What about glutathione soaps, lotions and tablets?

  • Soaps: rinsed off in seconds, so the glutathione has little time to act. Gentle washing helps; the soap itself is unlikely to change tan much. See serum vs cream vs soap.
  • Body lotions: a moisturising body lotion helps hands and feet look smoother and less dull, whatever is in it.
  • Tablets and injections: these are not a shortcut for tan. Evidence is limited and injections carry real risks. Read cream vs tablets vs injections and talk to a doctor before taking any supplement.

What to avoid

Lemon and sugar scrubs, bleach creams, baking soda pastes and unlabelled "whitening" body creams. Lemon on skin followed by sun can cause burns and darker patches. Some unlabelled creams contain steroids or mercury (Sonthalia, Daulatabad & Sarkar, IJDVL 2016). Harsh pumice on the tops of the feet also causes more darkness through friction. Hot-water soaks with detergent or bleach powder, sometimes suggested as a home pedicure, dry the skin badly and can cause burns.

When to see a doctor

A dark spot or mole on the hand, palm, sole or under a nail that is new, growing, changing colour or bleeding. Thick, velvety dark skin on the knuckles. Patches that itch, peel or spread. These need a doctor, not a cream. Also see a doctor if your hands or feet are darkening along with other changes, such as tiredness, weight loss or darkening of the gums, because rarely skin darkening can be a sign of a hormone or nutrition problem.
